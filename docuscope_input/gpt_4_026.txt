Denial of Justice and Legal Rights
Laborers had no legal recourse for most crimes committed against them.

Plantation owners were seen as above the law, and the colonial judicial system favored the interests of plantation management.

Crimes committed by plantation owners were rarely investigated, and even when there was some form of investigation, it was often biased and designed to protect the colonial economy rather than the workers.

The Judicial System
The judicial system in Suriname during the indenture period was deeply unjust and heavily skewed in favor of plantation owners.

Here are the key features:

1.

Colonial Control and Limited Legal Protection
Dutch colonial laws were designed to protect plantation interests, not the rights of the laborers.

Plantation owners had considerable influence over the legal system.

They could often bribe or coerce local officials to overlook crimes committed against indentured workers.

Indians and other laborers were treated as second-class citizens, and they had little to no access to the courts or legal protections.

They were frequently discriminated against, and their complaints were often dismissed.

2.

The Role of the Overseers and Plantations
Overseers, who were usually Dutch or European, had the power to impose punishments without any legal oversight.
