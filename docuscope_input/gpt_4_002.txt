The word "girmit" comes from a local pronunciation of the English word "agreement".

It originated during the 19th and early 20th centuries when Indian indentured laborers were sent to British colonies like Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, and others.

These laborers signed an indenture agreement (a contract) that bound them to work for a fixed period (usually 5 years) in return for wages, housing, and sometimes a return passage to India.

Because most of these workers spoke little or no English, the word "agreement" was phonetically adapted into "girmit" in their everyday speech.

As a result:

The workers themselves were often called "girmityas" (people bound by a girmit).

"Girmit" became a broader cultural term to refer to the entire indenture experience — the contracts, the voyage, the hardships, and the new communities formed abroad.

Resistance by indentured laborers in Suriname (then a Dutch colony) was a significant part of the history of indenture there.
