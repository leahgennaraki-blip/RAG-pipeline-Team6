The colonial records suggest that many were recruited under dubious circumstances, often misled or coerced into embarking on perilous journeys to foreign colonies.

Being single in a male-dominated immigrant community often branded them as individuals of "loose character," leading to social stigmatization.

The imbalanced sex ratio (fewer women than men) created further complexities, with single women subjected to unwanted advances and violence from overseers and male workers.

They were often targeted as sexual objects and faced harassment from figures of authority such as overseers and sirdars.

Despite these challenges, some single women exerted agency by navigating the constraints of the system, forming relationships strategically for economic and social stability.

### Married Women

Married women occupied a complex social position.

Legal recognition of marriages was often delayed or denied, particularly for traditional Hindu and Muslim marriages, complicating their legal standing and family life.

Upon marriage, they were often seen as subordinate, with limited economic and personal freedom.

Colonial policies aimed to incorporate European marriage ideals, promoting nuclear family structures, but this often conflicted with the realities on plantations.

Women were obliged to work alongside men in arduous plantation labor, often under duress, for meager wages.
