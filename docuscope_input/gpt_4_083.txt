The shared history of colonial exploitation and labor (both indentured and enslaved) creates a sense of solidarity, but also highlights the differences between these communities.

Younger generations of Indo-Surinamese people may identify more strongly with their Surinamese nationality than with their Indian heritage, viewing their identity through the lens of multiculturalism rather than strictly through the memory of indenture.

Conclusion
Memory plays a central role in shaping the identity of the descendants of indentured laborers, both at an individual and community level.

Through family stories, cultural practices, and intergenerational transmission, the memories of hardship, survival, and adaptation continue to inform how these generations perceive themselves and their place in society.

As these memories evolve, they shape a hybrid identity that blends both Indian cultural heritage and the experience of being Surinamese in a multicultural, post-colonial world.

For younger generations, the identity tied to the memories of indenture may be less personal and more symbolic, serving as a means to connect with the historical legacy of their ancestors while navigating modern societal contexts.
