These laborers had to sign an "agreement" before being transported to work on plantations in various colonies, such as Fiji, Mauritius, Trinidad, and others.

In their transliteration of the term into their native languages—which included Bhojpuri and other Indian dialects—the word "agreement" became "girmit."

The individuals who signed these agreements were referred to as "girmitiyas." This demotic form effectively symbolized their identity as people bound by the indenture contract.

"Girmit," therefore, wasn't just a word for the legal contract but became a broader term embodying the lived experience of the indentured laborers, encapsulating both the physical agreement and the harsh realities and conditions they faced under the system.

Moreover, the concept of "girmit" and "girmitiya" also served to establish a cultural and identity-driven connection among the laborers, facilitating group consciousness and solidarity in the context of shared struggle and resistance against the colonial plantation system.

It symbolized their collective identity and resistance, as noted in the historical accounts provided within the context.

The Mahatma Gandhi and other prominent figures used these terms to highlight the plight of indentured laborers in various parts of the British Empire.
