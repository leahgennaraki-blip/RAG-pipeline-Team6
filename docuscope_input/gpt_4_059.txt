Over time, these communities developed and grew into agricultural settlements, contributing to the food supply of Suriname and integrating into the broader economy.

They began to form a rural peasant class within the Indo-Surinamese population.

Despite this shift, many of the Indian laborers continued to face poverty and lacked access to the capital and resources that would have allowed them to become more successful landowners.

While the indentured system had ended, many still faced difficulties in breaking free from the legacy of exploitation and limited opportunities.

2.

The Rise of Indian Communities in Suriname
The descendants of the indentured laborers began to establish settlements and form communities in Suriname.

These communities began to develop their own identities and structures, relying on religion (Hinduism, Islam), caste and language (primarily Hindi, Bhojpuri, and Javanese) to maintain social cohesion.

Indian temples, mosques, and cultural institutions were established, and the Indian community started to develop its own distinct cultural and ethnic identity within the Surinamese nation.

3.

Economic and Social Disadvantage
While some indentured laborers were able to achieve economic stability through farming or trade, many others continued to struggle with poverty and lack of education.
