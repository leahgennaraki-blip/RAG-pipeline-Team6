It also focuses on the emotional and psychological impact of indenture, such as the trauma, loss, and sense of identity created by the experience.

Example:
The stories told by descendants of indentured laborers at family gatherings, community events, or religious ceremonies might describe the hardships their ancestors faced, as well as their resilience, dreams, and hopes.

These accounts can be more personal, emphasizing themes of family bonds, sacrifice, and the emotional costs of migration and labor.

For instance, the memory of a grandmother recounting her family’s journey from India or the stories of cultural survival in the face of adversity might be far more subjective than the written historical account.

Key Differences:
1.

Perspective and Interpretation:
Written History often provides a macro perspective, dealing with large-scale events and processes, while collective remembering offers a micro, more intimate and personalized view.

Written History might focus on facts, statistics, and analyses of labor policies, whereas collective remembering is about human experiences, emotional impact, and personal reflections.

2.

Transmission:
Written History is documented and published, typically by historians, scholars, or institutions.

It is disseminated through books, articles, journals, and official records.
