Diasporic: Indo-Surinamese people also identify with broader diasporic networks of Indian communities spread across the Caribbean, South America, and even globally, particularly with countries like Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, and Fiji, where indentured laborers were also sent.

3.

Politics of Identity in Suriname’s National Context
The politics of identity for Indo-Surinamese people has been shaped by a complex relationship between ethnicity, nationalism, and post-colonial power dynamics.

a.

Ethnic Politics and Division
Suriname’s political and social landscape has been heavily influenced by its ethnic diversity.

The rise of Afro-Surinamese nationalism in the 1960s-70s emphasized a vision of the nation rooted in African heritage and culture.

In contrast, Indo-Surinamese identity was often linked to Indian cultural traditions and the memory of indenture.

While both groups were united in their push for independence, post-independence nationalism in Suriname often exacerbated ethnic divisions, with Indo-Surinamese people feeling alienated from the broader nationalist movement that they felt was more focused on Afro-Surinamese identity.

The feeling of marginalization among Indo-Surinamese was particularly evident in the 1960s when political power was perceived to be shifting away from the Indo-Surinamese community (which had been influential in the colonial period) toward the Afro-Surinamese majority.
