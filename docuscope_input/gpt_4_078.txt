Memory and Identity Among Indentured Laborers
For the original indentured laborers, their experience in the indenture system profoundly shaped their personal and collective identities.

The shared hardships, survival tactics, and adaptation to a foreign land all became key components of how they saw themselves and how they were seen by others.

Memory played an essential role in forming a group identity among those who were taken from India and worked on plantations in places like Suriname.

Survival and Cultural Adaptation
The identity of indentured laborers was shaped by their memory of migration, the trauma of separation from their families, the harshness of plantation work, and the resilience they showed in surviving the grueling conditions.

These memories became foundational to their collective identity, which was centered around a shared struggle for survival, dignity, and cultural preservation.

As they were taken from their villages and communities in India, indentured laborers often found themselves displaced and alienated in a foreign land.

However, their memory of home, the values and customs they carried with them, became a form of cultural resilience that enabled them to hold onto their identity as Indians while adapting to their new environment.
