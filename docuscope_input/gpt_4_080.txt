These new generations navigate their identity within the context of modernity, national identity, and the complexities of being part of a multicultural society like Suriname.

a.

Family and Community Narratives
Much of the identity of the second and third generations of Indo-Surinamese people is shaped by family narratives passed down from the older generations.

These narratives often include memories of migration, the plantation life, and the struggles of their forebears.

In many Indo-Surinamese families, the memory of indenture is preserved through storytelling, which helps to reinforce cultural continuity and strengthen community bonds.

However, these memories are often distorted or romanticized over time.

For example, stories of hardship may become stories of triumph, or memories of suffering may be idealized as evidence of the family's resilience.

This selective remembering creates a collective identity that focuses more on the positive aspects of survival and adaptation rather than the pain and loss endured during the indenture period.

b.

Generational Shifts in Identity
New generations, particularly those who grew up in urbanized settings or are exposed to globalization and modern media, may feel detached from the actual lived experiences of their ancestors.
