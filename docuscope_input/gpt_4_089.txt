Conclusion
The Dutch government has yet to fully acknowledge the violence and systemic abuse faced by indentured laborers in Suriname, nor has it made formal attempts at reparations.

However, growing recognition of the need to address the historical wrongs of both slavery and indenture is part of an ongoing discourse.

This issue remains tied to questions of national identity, historical justice, and the long-term impact of colonial practices on the descendants of those who were exploited.

The demand for reparations by the descendants of indentured laborers in Suriname (and in other former colonies) is a multifaceted and evolving issue.

It involves both collective calls for official recognition of the injustice and individual accounts that highlight the personal impact of the indenture system and the subsequent legacy.

These demands are rooted in a sense of historical wrongs, the belief that the exploitation suffered by indentured laborers and their families is an ongoing injustice, and the economic, social, and cultural impacts that continue to affect the descendants of indentured workers.

Individual accounts from those in the Indo-Surinamese community provide a human face to these demands and show how they have evolved over time.
