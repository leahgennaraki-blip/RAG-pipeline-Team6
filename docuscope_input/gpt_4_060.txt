The system of indenture had prevented many workers from gaining the skills necessary to access higher-paying work, and the community faced the lingering effects of an underprivileged status.

This economic challenge was exacerbated by racial and social discrimination from both the colonial system and the broader Surinamese society, where the Indo-Surinamese population often faced prejudice from both Afro-Surinamese and European populations.

Many of the Indian workers continued to face economic exploitation in the form of wage labor on the same sugar plantations or in other sectors, though by now they were no longer bound by indenture contracts.

4.

Political and Social Mobilization
As the indentured system ended, the Indian community in Suriname began to find its political voice.

Initially, many of the indentured laborers were not politically active, as they were subject to strict control by plantation owners and colonial authorities.

However, by the mid-20th century, Suriname's Indo-Surinamese population started to demand better political representation and rights.

Labor movements and political activism grew in the Indian community in Suriname, especially in the 1930s and 1940s.
