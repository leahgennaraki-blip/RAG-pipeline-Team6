In Summary:
The "lang" system in Suriname was a coercive extension of the indenture contract that allowed plantation owners to keep laborers working beyond the initial agreed period, often under exploitative and harsh conditions.

It was a mechanism that kept indentured laborers in servitude even after they had completed their original contracts, creating a form of indentured slavery that persisted in practice until the full abolition of the system.

The concept of identity is intricately tied to memory, particularly in the context of indentured labor, as it involves the intersection of individual and collective memories of hardship, survival, adaptation, and resilience.

The memories of indentured laborers are not only preserved through the stories they passed down but are also an essential part of shaping their descendants' understanding of their heritage, community, and belonging.

This intergenerational transmission of memory contributes to how identity is formed and evolves across generations, especially in societies like Suriname, where the descendants of Indian indentured laborers form an integral part of the national identity.
