A central theme in the novel is the concept of *jahaji bhai* (ship-brother) and *jahaji bahin* (ship-sister), terms used by indentured Indians to denote the deep bonds formed with fellow travelers during their voyage across the "kala pani", or dark waters, to the Caribbean.

These bonds were pivotal, forming a new familial framework for survival and solidarity in an unfamiliar land.

The novel is partly autobiographical, as Mohan reveals through the narrator's encounters with the elderly Bhojpuri-speaking woman, Deeda, whose personal stories and folktales serve as a conduit to the past.

These sessions, taped by the narrator in the 1970s, underscore the importance of oral traditions in preserving history.

The Bhojpuri narratives shared by Deeda often feature themes of women's independence and resilience, challenging typical patriarchal norms embedded in traditional folktales.

*Jahajin* also addresses the limitations and responsibilities inherent in the act of translation, both linguistic and cultural.

While the narrator attempts to connect younger generations of Indian-Trinidadians with their heritage, she faces the risk of "vicarious victimhood", where the lines between empathy and identification become blurred.

This self-conscious effort to memorialize and excavate women's narratives and agency serves as a form of feminist cultural resistance.
