The indentured labour system in Suriname ended formally in 1916, but its social, economic, and psychological effects shaped the Indian (Hindostani) population for decades afterward.

The end of indenture was not abrupt liberation; it was a managed withdrawal of coercion, followed by a long and uneven transition to freedom, settlement, and citizenship.

Below is a clear account of when, how, and with what consequences indenture ended in Suriname.

1.

When Indenture Ended

1873: Arrival of the first Indian indentured labourers in Suriname

1916: Official termination of Indian indenture

The end date aligns with the broader abolition of Indian indenture across the British imperial system, even though Suriname was a Dutch colony.

This was possible because Indian labour recruitment depended on British-controlled India.

2.

Why Indenture Ended

Indenture ended due to sustained pressure from multiple directions, not colonial benevolence.

a.

Indian Nationalist Opposition

In India, leaders, reformers, and journalists increasingly condemned indenture as:

A disguised form of slavery

Morally degrading

Violent and exploitative

Public campaigns exposed:

Sexual abuse of women

Criminalization of labourers

High mortality and imprisonment rates

This created political pressure on the British government to halt recruitment.
