Historical records, such as John Herlein's reports, noted that legal measures attempted to restrict such relationships.

For example, Van Sommelsdijck's 1686 planting by-law expressly forbade carnal intimacy between European men and Black women or free Indian women, initially penalizing offenders with a fine of two pounds of sugar—a penalty that was later increased to 200 florins and eventually made conditional on plantation disturbances by 1784.

By 1817, the specific article was rescinded, signifying a shift in attitudes or enforcement challenges.

### Social Dynamics

1.

**White Men and Black Women:**
Though officially interracial marriages were prohibited, interracial cohabitation was widespread, effectively circumventing the legal bans on interracial marriages.

Interracial concubinage provided Black and colored women with a measure of material security and social standing, despite never being fully equitable or stable, as they were often left behind when their partners returned to Europe.

Many white men manumitted their Black partners, tacitly acknowledging these relationships in a socially acceptable framework within the context of their colonial power structures.

2.

**White Women and Black Men:**
Such relationships were viewed extremely negatively and provoked severe social and legal penalties.
