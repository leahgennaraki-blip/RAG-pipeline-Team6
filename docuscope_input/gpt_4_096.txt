In the post-colonial context, nationalism became a central part of constructing the new nation, which sought to create a sense of unity among its diverse population, including Afro-Surinamese, Indo-Surinamese, Chinese, and indigenous groups.

However, the diversity of Suriname’s population meant that ethnic and cultural identities often intersected with the larger nationalist project, creating both opportunities for integration and tensions surrounding recognition and representation.

2.

Identity of the Indo-Surinamese Community
For Indo-Surinamese people, the legacy of indenture plays a critical role in shaping their identity.

They find themselves at the intersection of several identities:

Surinamese: As citizens of Suriname, Indo-Surinamese people identify strongly with the nation’s political and cultural life.

Their experiences as Indians in Suriname, particularly after independence, are shaped by their integration into the national fabric, and many have adopted Suriname’s official language (Dutch) and participate in the national culture.

Indian: Despite their long history in Suriname, many Indo-Surinamese maintain a deep cultural affinity with India, particularly through religion (Hinduism and Islam), language (Bhojpuri, Hindi), and cultural practices.

This connection to India is more symbolic and cultural than political, but it plays a significant role in how the community perceives itself.
