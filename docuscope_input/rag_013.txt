For instance, in many colonies, a free return passage was offered only after completing a second term of indenture.

This created a situation where many laborers were effectively encouraged or compelled to remain in the colonies longer than initially anticipated.

### Actual Returns and Obstacles
Despite the contractual provisions, a significant number of laborers did not return to India.

Official records suggest that by the 1870s, about 21% of the indentured migrants had returned, and in subsequent decades, the likelihood of return diminished, with one returning for every two who migrated.

By the late 1910s, nearly 40% of indentured laborers opted for repatriation.

However, several factors impeded their ability to do so:

1.

**Economic and Personal Realities**:
- Many laborers found new opportunities or gained a sense of personal freedom that was not available to them in India.

- Some prospered economically, which may have influenced their decision to stay, while others remained impoverished and could not afford to return, despite savings accrued during their indenture contracts.

2.

**Discouragement and Inertia**:
- Colonial authorities often discouraged repatriation, seeing the laborers as an essential workforce.
