Unlike the Indians, Chinese workers often married within their own group, resulting in a more cohesive social structure in the early years of their settlement.

4.

Post-Indenture Treatment and Legacy
Indian Laborers:
After their indenture contracts expired, many Indians in Suriname opted to remain in the country, either by buying land or by working as small-scale farmers.

They were restricted to rural areas and continued to face prejudice.

Indians continued to dominate the sugar industry as their communities expanded.

Over time, many became economically successful, especially in agriculture, though they still faced discrimination from the Dutch and Chinese.

Chinese Laborers:
Many Chinese workers did not stay after their contracts ended and returned to China, though some intermarried with other groups, including the Indian laborers, forming mixed communities.

A small number of Chinese remained in Suriname and integrated into commercial and administrative life.

By the early 20th century, many had become shopkeepers, artisans, and entrepreneurs.

5.

Racial Hierarchies and Discrimination
Both Indian and Chinese laborers faced discrimination from Dutch authorities, but the discrimination was often racialized differently.

The Indian laborers were often treated as "lower caste" and were subjected to harsher working conditions, especially as they were brought in greater numbers.
