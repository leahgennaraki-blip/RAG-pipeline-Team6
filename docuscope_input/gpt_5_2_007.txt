In Summary

Indentured labourers in Suriname were not passive victims.

Through daily defiance, legal action, desertion, cultural persistence, and open rebellion, they consistently challenged colonial power.

Their resistance reshaped the indenture system and laid the groundwork for post-indenture political, cultural, and social life.

The income of indentured labourers was deliberately kept at subsistence level and varied by colony, time period, and plantation type.

Across the indenture system (c.

1834–1917), wages were structured to meet minimal survival needs while maintaining planter profitability and labour control.

Below is a clear, historically grounded breakdown.

Contractual Wages (Nominal)

For Indian indentured labourers in the British and Dutch Caribbean, Fiji, and Mauritius, standard wages were broadly similar.

Typical Daily Wages

Men: 20–25 cents (colonial currency) per working day

Women: 12–16 cents per working day

Children: Paid less or unpaid

This usually translated to:

Monthly income: roughly the equivalent of ₹2–₹4 in late-19th-century Indian rupees

Annual income: enough only for basic food, clothing, and hut maintenance

Women were consistently paid less despite doing comparable field labour.
