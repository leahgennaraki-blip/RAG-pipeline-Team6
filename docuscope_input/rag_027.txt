In the context of colonial labor, it was a derogatory term used globally to describe Asian workers involved in menial, contract labor.

The term carried pejorative connotations from the start and was meant to dehumanize and homogenize the diverse identities of these laborers into a single, derogatory category.

In China, the term was transliterated as "kuli," meaning "bitter strength," which highlights the arduous and oppressive conditions under which they worked.

- **Indentured Labourer**: An indentured laborer, in contrast, was someone who entered into a contractual agreement or "girmit" (a corruption of "agreement" in Hindi) to work for a specified period, typically several years, in return for passage, sustenance, and a nominal wage.

The indenture system arose after the abolition of slavery, aiming to provide a steady supply of laborers for colonial plantations.

**Historical Context**:
- The indenture system began in the early 19th century as a replacement for slave labor, with indentured laborers often working under harsh conditions similar to slavery.

However, unlike slaves, indentured laborers were contractually bound for a limited period, and in theory, they or their descendants were not considered property and were entitled to some rights, including eventual freedom and repatriation options.
