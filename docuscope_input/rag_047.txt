This form of protest was seen as a defiance of the labor systems and an attempt to escape the oppressive conditions, sometimes integrating into emerging free communities or smaller, less regulated settings on plantation fringes.

3.

**Strikes**: While collective action was difficult due to systemic constraints, strikes did occur.

These were typically short-lived and concerned specific grievances like excessive workloads or unfair wage practices.

For instance, the strikes in the Rewa region in the 1880s addressed increases in task burdens.

4.

**Direct Confrontation**: Some laborers resorted to more direct forms of protest, such as attacking overseers, drivers, or even significant figures within the plantation structure.

These confrontations could involve physical violence and were often spontaneous reactions to immediate abuses or provocations.

5.

**Petitions and Appeals**: Some indentured laborers sought redress through formal channels, like petitions to colonial governments, although these were frequently dismissed or ignored due to racial and administrative biases.

An example is Karim Bux's direct appeal to the governor in 1912, which was ultimately unaddressed despite its humble tone.
