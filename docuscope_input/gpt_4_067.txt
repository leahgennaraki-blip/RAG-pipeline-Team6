Collective remembering is primarily transmitted through oral traditions, family storytelling, and cultural practices.

It is passed down through generations in informal, non-institutional settings, such as community gatherings or religious ceremonies.

3.

Inclusivity and Representation:
Written History can be dominated by the perspectives of those in power, such as colonial authorities or plantation owners, leading to a limited or biased account of indenture.

Collective remembering, however, often emphasizes the voices of the indentured laborers themselves or their descendants, offering a more inclusive, though subjective, representation of their lived experiences.

4.

Emotional vs.

Analytical Focus:
Written History tends to have an analytical tone, focusing on facts and evidence, and aiming for objectivity.

Collective remembering is more emotionally driven, centered around feelings of resilience, suffering, pride, and identity.

It is often less concerned with factual accuracy and more with preserving a sense of belonging and community memory.

5.

Cultural Preservation:
Written History may offer a static account of past events, whereas collective remembering evolves over time.

It is fluid, changing with each generation, reflecting the shifting cultural, political, and social contexts.

Through collective remembering, communities often reinterpret history to meet their current needs, values, and identities.
