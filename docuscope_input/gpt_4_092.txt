Many feel that the legacy of indenture has left them in a marginalized economic position, and they see reparations as a way to redress the imbalance caused by centuries of exploitation.

a.

Economic Disparities
Vikram, an Indo-Surinamese businessman, argues that the economic opportunities available to his family have always been restricted.

He points out that while Afro-Surinamese communities have had access to state-sponsored land and business opportunities since independence, Indo-Surinamese communities have faced more challenges in securing economic equality.

"My ancestors worked on the sugar plantations without pay.

They died in the fields, and their descendants are still struggling to own land, to be seen as equals in the business world," he says.

Economic reparations, for people like Vikram, mean not just compensation for past wrongs but also a leveling of the playing field.

They call for access to grants, education, and business development programs that would enable future generations to have the same opportunities as other groups in Suriname.

b.

Financial Redress for Social Marginalization
The idea of financial reparations extends beyond individual compensation and calls for state-sponsored programs that address the systemic barriers Indo-Surinamese people face in terms of social mobility.
