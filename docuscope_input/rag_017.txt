These testimonios reveal the brutal conditions endured by indentured laborers: cramped ship quarters, insufficient food (often described as dog-biscuits, sugar, rice, and dried fish), and the dehumanizing nature of life in the colonies, where they were subjected to racial segregation and severe labor conditions.

Women, in particular, faced harsh realities, being seen both as productive workers and subject to exploitation and oppressive gender norms.

Despite this, they played crucial roles in maintaining cultural practices and the social fabric of their communities.

Resistance was part of the indenture experience.

Some laborers resisted individually through work slowdowns or fleeing, while collective efforts were less common but notable, such as the Gandhi-led strike in 1913 in Natal.

The resilience and agency shown by the indentured are important facets of their stories, suggesting that while many were misled into indenture, they were not passive victims.

Rather, they sought opportunities for betterment, away from the rigid caste structures and economic hardships of India.

While official inquiries, like the Sanderson Committee, often downplayed the abuses of the system, the voices from testimonies tell a different story.
