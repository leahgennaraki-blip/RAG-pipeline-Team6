Peggy Mohan’s Jahajin is a historical and semi-autobiographical novel that interweaves personal narrative, oral history and cultural memory to portray the Indian indenture experience in Trinidad, with a particular focus on the journeys and agency of women known as jahajins (literally “ship-sisters”).

Goodreads

Core Premise:
The story is framed through the perspective of a young Trinidad-born linguist of Indian descent conducting research on Bhojpuri, the dialect brought by Indian migrants to the Caribbean.

In the course of her work she interviews Deeda, a 110-year-old jahajin who traveled from her village in Basti, northern India, to Trinidad with her young son as an indentured laborer on sugar plantations.

Through Deeda’s recollections, the narrator reconstructs the remarkable journey from India to “Chini-dad” (the name Indians gave Trinidad for its sugar), the settlement and community life that followed, and the cultural transformations that unfolded over generations.

Narrative Structure and Themes:

Migration and Indenture: The novel opens with the train ride from Faizabad to Calcutta, followed by a three-month voyage around the Cape of Good Hope to Trinidad—a journey that is both physically arduous and culturally dislocating.
